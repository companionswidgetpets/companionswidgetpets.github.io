Few fish carry as much meaning as the koi. In Japan and China a carp stands for perseverance, success and good fortune, and in Japanese its very name sounds like love. Here is where those meanings come from, what the colours are said to mean, and how to keep a little koi pond where you'll see it every day.

## The legend of the Dragon Gate

The story behind it all is Chinese. Carp, it says, swim up the Yellow River against the current until they reach a great waterfall called the Dragon Gate. Most give up. The few that leap all the way to the top are turned into dragons.

So a carp became the symbol of effort rewarded: of the student who passes the hard exam, the worker who rises against the odds. In Chinese, "a carp leaping over the Dragon Gate" still means a great success after a long struggle, and in Japan the same picture, a carp climbing a waterfall, stands for getting on in life through perseverance.

## Koi in Japan

The colourful koi people keep in ponds today are Japanese. They are nishikigoi, "brocaded carp", first bred from ordinary carp by rice farmers in the mountains of Niigata in the 1800s, and refined over two centuries into dozens of named varieties.

Every spring, for Children's Day on 5 May, Japanese families fly koinobori: carp-shaped streamers on a pole, one for each child, wishing them the strength of a carp swimming upstream. Seeing a whole river valley of them flapping in the wind is one of the happiest sights of the year.

And there is the pun. In Japanese, koi (鯉), carp, sounds exactly like koi (恋), romantic love. Two koi swimming together are an easy symbol for a couple.

## Fish and good fortune in China

In Chinese, the word for fish, yú (鱼), sounds the same as the word for "surplus", yú (余). That is why a fish is served at Lunar New Year and why pictures of fish hang on doors: "may you have plenty, year after year" is a pun on fish. The word for carp sounds close to the word for profit, and goldfish, jīnyú, sound almost like "gold and jade".

Feng shui traditions often recommend an aquarium for prosperity. A popular arrangement is nine fish: eight golden ones and one black, said to absorb bad luck.

## What koi colours are said to mean

Many colour meanings come from modern koi keepers and tattoo artists rather than old tradition, but they are widely shared:

- **Gold** (the gold koi, Ogon in Japanese): wealth and prosperity. Gold fish have meant money in Chinese for centuries.
- **Red and white** (Kohaku): red and white together are Japan's colours of celebration, used for weddings, festivals and even the country's big New Year's Eve song contest. A Kohaku koi is a happy, lucky fish.
- **Black**: overcoming hard times, and change for the better.
- **Blue**: calm and peace.

## Koi facts worth knowing

- **They live a long time.** Pond koi often live 25 to 35 years. A famous scarlet koi named Hanako was said to be 226 years old when she died in 1977, judged from the rings on her scales, though many scientists doubt the figure.
- **They grow big.** A well-kept koi can reach 60 to 90 centimetres.
- **They know who feeds them.** Koi learn to recognise their keepers and will take food from your hand.
- **A koi is a carp.** Nishikigoi are a domesticated, decorative form of the common carp.

## Questions people ask

### What does a koi fish symbolise?

Perseverance and success, from the Chinese legend of the carp that leaps the Dragon Gate and becomes a dragon. In Japan, koi also stand for strength (on Children's Day) and, through a pun, for love.

### Are koi good luck?

In Chinese and Japanese tradition, yes. Fish in general stand for abundance in Chinese, and carp for success through effort.

### What does a gold koi mean?

Wealth and prosperity. Gold fish have been symbols of money and good fortune in China for centuries.

### How long do koi live?

Usually 25 to 35 years in a well-kept pond, and sometimes far longer.
